## The one-line summary

Gradient descent gave you the *loop*, but "the model" was still a black box. Here's the whole of
it: **a model is just a big pile of numbers — weights — and the smallest model is a single
<Term id="neuron">neuron</Term>.** Training nudges those weights (real gradient descent); what they
encode is a **rule**; and stacking neurons into a **<Term id="neural network">network</Term>**, tuned
by **<Term id="backpropagation">backprop</Term>**, is how that rule gets complex. Once you see that
the model *is its weights*, the operational facts fall out — what a checkpoint saves, why training is
so much more memory-hungry than inference, and why the two run on different fleets.

<Callout type="tip">
**Infra bridge.** You already manage systems that score inputs with weighted rules — a weighted
health-check that decides in/out of rotation, a risk score from several signals. A neuron is exactly
that, with one twist: **the weights are learned, not hand-tuned.** Training is the process that sets
them.
</Callout>

## A neuron is a weighted vote

A neuron takes each input, multiplies it by a learned **<Term id="weight">weight</Term>** (how much
it listens to that input), adds a **<Term id="bias">bias</Term>** (its baseline), and squashes the
result with an **<Term id="activation function">activation function</Term>** — here a
**<Term id="sigmoid">sigmoid</Term>**, which maps anything to a 0–1 score. Past 0.5 it says one class;
below, the other. That's **<Term id="classification">classification</Term>**.

two weights and the bias until the line cleanly separates "buy" from "walk," then predict a new car:

The line it finds is the **<Term id="decision boundary">decision boundary</Term>** — the rule the
neuron learned, drawn in input space. Nothing was hand-coded; the weights *became* the rule.

## The weights ARE the model

This is the part worth internalizing. The three numbers in that sim — `w₁`, `w₂`, `b` — are the
entire model. Training changed nothing else. Scale the idea up and a real model is the same thing:
millions to billions of weights, just a big <Term id="tensor">tensor</Term> of numbers (the
**<Term id="parameters">parameters</Term>**).

<Callout type="tip">
**Infra bridge.** "The weights are the model" is the fact behind your whole operational picture:
- a **checkpoint** is literally a dump of these numbers — that's what you save and restore;
- the weights are the tensor that **fills HBM** — "a 70B model" means 70 billion of them (≈140 GB in
  bf16 before you've stored a single activation);
- "loading the model" = streaming those numbers onto the chips.
The model isn't code plus data — it's *this array of learned numbers*.
</Callout>

## One neuron draws a straight line — that's its ceiling

A single neuron can only ever carve a **straight** decision boundary. Give it data that no straight
line can split and it will never get the loss to zero — it's not under-trained, it's *under-powered*.
The fix isn't more epochs; it's more neurons. A straight line is what a purely *linear* rule gives
you — and stacking linear rules just yields another straight line. The activation (the sigmoid
squash) makes each neuron **nonlinear**, and *that's* what lets a stack of them combine into a bent,
curved boundary. That stack is a **neural network**.

## How a network learns — backpropagation

Now the real thing: inputs feed a **<Term id="hidden layer">hidden layer</Term>** of neurons, which
feeds the output. More weights, so a harder question — *when the guess is wrong, which weight gets the
blame?* The answer is **backpropagation**. Run the forward pass, measure the error at the output, then
send that error **backward**: each neuron gets a share of the blame — written **δ** ("delta"), its
slice of the error — scaled by how much it contributed (its weight to the output). Every weight then
takes the same downhill step from the last topic — **gradient descent, applied to each weight
individually.**

Notice the connection **thickness** tracks weight magnitude: training grows the paths that matter and

<Callout type="tip">
**Infra bridge.** Backprop is the reason **training's memory footprint dwarfs inference's.** To send
the error backward, the chip must *keep the forward activations* it computed on the way in — every
layer's intermediate tensors stay resident until the backward pass uses them. Add the optimizer's
per-parameter state — **Adam, the standard optimizer, keeps two extra numbers per weight (a running
average of past gradients and their square), about 8 bytes per parameter in fp32; with an fp32
master copy of the weights plus bf16 weights and gradients, mixed-precision training holds ~16 bytes
per parameter** — and a training step needs *several times* the HBM of just running the model. Inference
keeps none of it — one forward pass, then discard. That gap is why an OOM-free serving config can
still OOM the moment you try to train on it — and why a training job that **OOMs mid-run rather than
at startup** points at activations first: their footprint grows with batch size and sequence length,
not just model size.
</Callout>

## Train once, infer forever

Two very different jobs share the same network:

- **Training** — the slow loop (forward → backprop → update), millions of steps, holding activations
  and optimizer state. Expensive, stateful, long-running. You do it once (or rarely).
- **Inference** — a single forward pass on new input. Cheap, stateless, fast. You do it constantly.

<Callout type="tip">
**Infra bridge.** This asymmetry is why training and serving run on **separate fleets** sized
differently: training clusters are big, checkpoint-heavy, and tolerant of long jobs; serving fleets
are latency-tuned and replicated for throughput. You pay a huge one-time cost to set the weights, then
serve cheap forever — and at scale the *serving* bill still dominates because you run inference so
many more times, so a deployment's cost model should track training and serving spend separately.
</Callout>

## Why this matters

- **"The model" is a pile of weights.** That's what a checkpoint is, what loads onto the chips, and
  what the parameter count measures — so weights/HBM math (last lesson's [What Is a
  Tensor?](/topics/math-infra/what-is-a-tensor)) is how you size it.
- **Backprop explains the training memory bill.** Stored activations + optimizer state are *why*
  training needs multiples of the model's own size in HBM — and why a config that serves fine can't
  necessarily train.
- **Train vs infer is a cost split, not a detail.** Different fleets, different sizing, different
  failure modes — all because one job runs the loop and the other runs a single pass.
