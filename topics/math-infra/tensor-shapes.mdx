## The one-line summary

The most baffling failure you'll hit operating ML jobs is the **mysterious OOM**: a run that
"should fit" by your back-of-envelope math dies anyway. The cause is almost always **shape, not
size**. Accelerators are *rigid* — the matrix unit works in **fixed-size tiles** (commonly
**128×128**), and the compiler **silently zero-pads** any dimension that isn't a multiple of the
tile. A 129-wide matrix quietly becomes 256-wide: you pay compute and memory for a wall of zeros.
Knowing this turns "why did it OOM?" into a ten-second diagnosis.

<Callout type="tip">
**Run it yourself.** A short [Colab notebook](https://colab.research.google.com/github/thebobrovs/LMS-content/blob/main/notebooks/tensor-shapes.ipynb)
computes the padding tax and charts the **"sawtooth of waste"** — zero at every multiple of the tile
(128 here), a cliff just past it (129 → 256 ≈ +98%). Pure `numpy`, runs on **CPU**, no GPU.
</Callout>

## Shape is what gets allocated

You already know a **<Term id="tensor">tensor</Term>** is just a multidimensional array of numbers
(see [What Is a Tensor?](/topics/math-infra/what-is-a-tensor)). What matters *here* is its
**shape** — the size of each dimension. Shape, not a vague "size," sets the memory footprint and
decides how the tensor maps onto the hardware.

<Callout type="tip">
**Infra bridge.** Treat the shape like a struct's field sizes or a packet's fixed layout: the
*dimensions*, not a hand-waved byte count, decide what gets allocated. Two tensors with the same
element count but different shapes can cost very different amounts of memory — and that gap is the
whole topic.
</Callout>

## The hardware tile, and the zeros you didn't ask for

An accelerator's <Term id="mxu">matrix unit</Term> processes the world in **fixed-size tiles** —
most commonly **128×128** (some use 256). **We'll use 128 throughout**; your accelerator may use a
different size, but the rule is identical. When a matmul dimension isn't a multiple of that tile, the
compiler **<Term id="padding">pads</Term>** it up to the next multiple. So a `129`-wide
dimension spills past the first 128-tile into a second — rounding up to 2×128 = `256`, and that whole
second tile is almost entirely zeros the hardware still stores and still multiplies. (The vector
unit's **lanes** — its parallel slots, like SIMD lanes — also want filling in multiples of **8**, a
smaller version of the same tax; but the headline rule is the matrix tile.)

## Padding costs compute *and* memory — and the memory is the OOM

Two bills come due. The **compute** bill: the matrix unit does real FLOPs on the padded zeros (a
129→256 dimension nearly doubles that axis's work). The **memory** bill: those zeros are *stored* in
<Term id="hbm">HBM</Term> — and *that* is the surprise OOM. You sized the job by its real element
count; the device allocated the padded shape, which is bigger. The job that fit on your napkin
doesn't fit on the device. Put bluntly: at a near-tile dimension you're renting a device you're only
half-using.

That's why the same byte-width-and-intensity <Term id="roofline">roofline</Term> from the precision
lesson is sensitive to shape too — padding inflates the bytes moved. Set a clean dimension and note

## Why shapes are rigid (and why they recompile)

One more thing that trips up engineers coming from dynamic Python: accelerator shapes are
**static**, fixed at compile time. The device can't allocate on the fly — it needs the exact buffer
layout planned *before* it runs. So shapes freeze, and **changing one re-triggers compilation**
(the "why did my step suddenly get slow after I changed the batch size?" gotcha).

<Callout type="tip">
**Infra bridge.** Static shapes are pre-allocated fixed buffers — faster than malloc-on-the-fly,
but the price is that a new shape invalidates the plan and forces a recompile. It's the same trade
as a statically-sized ring buffer vs. a dynamic allocator.
</Callout>

## Why this matters

Shape is one of the few performance-and-capacity levers you can reason about without touching the
model. When a data scientist files "my job OOMs and it shouldn't," your first move is now
**"are the batch and feature dims aligned to the hardware tile?"** — assume **128** unless the
accelerator's docs or the compiler's padding warnings say otherwise. And when someone's MFU is
mysteriously low, padding on a near-tile dimension is a prime suspect. You won't
pick the model's dimensions — but you can spot the ones that quietly cost double, flag them to the
team that owns the model, and turn a guessed capacity plan into a sized one.
