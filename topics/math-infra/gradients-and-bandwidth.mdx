## The one-line summary

On one accelerator, training has **no network cost**. Spread it across many and a tax appears that
dominates at scale: **every step, each device computes a <Term id="gradient">gradient</Term> the size
of the whole model, and they must all be summed together before the next step.** That sync is an
**<Term id="all-reduce">all-reduce</Term>**, it rides the **<Term id="interconnect">interconnect</Term>**
between accelerators, and its time is simply **bytes ÷ bandwidth**. Get a feel for when that sync
overtakes the math and you'll know the thing that trips up every first cluster: past a point, **adding
devices stops making training faster.**

<Callout type="tip">
**Infra bridge.** You already budget for recurring, fixed-size transfers — a replication stream, a
log shipment every interval. A gradient is exactly that: a blob the size of the model, moved across the
fabric *every single step*. The only question is whether the fabric can move it faster than the chips
can produce the next one.
</Callout>

## A gradient is a payload, not a derivative

Forget the calculus. Operationally, a gradient is just **a second copy of the model** — one number per
parameter, saying which way to nudge that weight. A 7B-parameter model in `bf16` produces a **~14 GB**
gradient. Every step. On every device. (That's the same byte math as [precision &
memory](/topics/math-infra/precision-and-memory): params × bytes-per-number.)

The catch of multi-device training: each device sees only its slice of the data, so it computes a
*different* gradient. Before anyone can take a step, those gradients must be **averaged into one** — so
every device stays on the same model. That reconciliation is the network cost.

## All-reduce: the sync, and bytes-on-the-wire is the cost

The collective that does it is **all-reduce**: sum every device's gradient, hand each device the
total. It rides the fast interconnect (not the slow cross-node network), and a **ring** all-reduce is
clever — each device passes its payload around the loop in small segments, and as you add devices each
segment shrinks proportionally. So the data each device moves stays roughly **2× the payload**
*regardless of how many devices there are* — the cost doesn't blow up as you scale. But the time for
that pass is still just **bytes ÷ bandwidth**.

So the whole step is two phases: **compute** the gradients (the backward pass), then **all-reduce**
them. Drive the two against each other below — set the model size, the device count, and the

<Callout type="tip">
**Infra bridge.** All-reduce is a cluster-wide **reduce/aggregate** — like rolling per-shard counters
into a global total every tick. The bottleneck isn't the arithmetic; it's the interconnect moving the
partial sums around. That's why it runs on the fast fabric and why its cost is measured in *bandwidth*,
not FLOPs.
</Callout>

## When adding devices stops helping — the communication wall

Here's the trap. Hold the **global batch** fixed — the total examples processed per step across all
devices — and add devices: each device now does **less compute** (it owns a smaller slice of that
batch), so per-device compute time *shrinks*. But the all-reduce moves
the same model-sized payload — its time stays **flat**. Shrinking one phase while the other holds means
the sync's *share* of every step keeps rising, until it **dominates**. At that point you're
**<Term id="communication-bound">communication-bound</Term>**: doubling the devices barely moves the
needle, and the speedup curve in the sim peels away from the ideal line and flattens.

<Callout type="tip">
**Infra bridge.** This is **Amdahl's law / the serial-section problem** you know from any parallel
system: once the un-parallelizable part (here, the sync) dominates, more workers stop helping and can
even hurt (coordination overhead). "We added a rack and throughput barely moved" is this curve, live.
</Callout>

## The levers (and the one that backfires)

When a run is communication-bound, three things actually help — and one tempting move makes it worse:

- **Overlap comm with compute.** The backward pass produces gradients **one layer at a time** (last
  layer first), so each layer's gradient is ready to all-reduce the moment that layer finishes — you can
  start syncing the early ones while later ones are still computing, hiding the sync *behind* compute
  instead of adding to it. Flip the **overlap** toggle in the sim and watch the step time drop; it's
  automatic in most frameworks.
- **A faster interconnect.** Sync time is bytes ÷ bandwidth, so more bandwidth (a fatter fabric, or
  keeping the job on the fast in-node interconnect instead of the slow cross-node network) directly
  cuts it.
- **Bigger steps** (more compute per sync) — a larger per-device batch raises the compute side, so the
  fixed sync becomes a smaller share of each step.
- **✗ More devices** — the reflex, and the wrong one once you're comm-bound: it shrinks compute while
  the sync stays flat, *raising* the sync's share.

## Why this matters

- **Provisioning.** "Throw more chips at it" has a ceiling, and this is where it is. Past the wall you're
  paying for devices that mostly wait on the network — the scaling-efficiency number in the sim is the
  one to watch.
- **The interconnect is a first-class resource.** Training cares about *bandwidth between* accelerators,
  not just FLOPs and HBM — which is why the fast in-node fabric and the network topology (the next topic)
  matter so much, and why jobs are packed to stay on it.
- **Reading a stalled scale-up.** "We doubled the pod and throughput barely moved" is no longer a mystery
  — it's the comm wall, and the fix is overlap or bandwidth, not more devices.
