## Why model choice matters early

The model fixes much of an agent's cost, its latency, where its data is
processed and how far you can customise it. It is also hard to change later:
prompts, tool descriptions and evaluation thresholds all get tuned to one model's
behaviour. So make the choice deliberately, from requirements, not from habit.

The exam frames it as three comparisons: a large or a small model, an open or a
proprietary model, and a model you host or one you consume as a service. They are
three separate decisions, and the most common mistake is to treat two of them as
one.

## Three separate decisions

**Size.** A large model reasons better over long, multi-step problems. A
<Term id="SLM">small language model (SLM)</Term> answers faster and costs less
per request, but handles a narrower range of tasks well. Within Gemini, the Pro,
Flash and Flash-Lite tiers are steps along this axis: the same family and the same
API, trading capability for speed and cost.

**Openness.** A proprietary model, such as Gemini or a partner model like
Claude, is used through its provider's service; you never hold the weights. An
open-weights model, such as Google's Gemma, publishes its weights, so you may
fine-tune it, inspect it and run it on hardware you choose. Tuning alone doesn't
force open weights: Gemini models can be tuned through Agent Platform's managed
tuning, but you never hold the result. Choose open weights when you need to hold
the model yourself, for example to tune it and run it on your own machines.

**Serving.** With <Term id="MaaS">model as a service (MaaS)</Term> you call a
managed endpoint and pay as you go (reserved capacity is an option), with
nothing to operate. With a
<Term id="self-deployed model">self-deployed model</Term> you deploy the weights
to an endpoint or cluster you control, such as an Agent Platform endpoint or
GKE, and you pay for that compute while it is provisioned, usually whether
or not requests arrive.
<Term id="Model Garden">Model Garden</Term> is the catalogue where you find
Google, partner and open models and the serving options each one offers.

<Callout type="warning">Open does not mean self-hosted. Model Garden offers some open models, Gemma among them, as managed MaaS endpoints too. Open weights give you the option to self-deploy, not the obligation.</Callout>

## From requirement to choice

Each axis is pushed by different requirements. Read the requirement, find the
axis it acts on, then choose.

Two rows deserve a closer look:

- **Residency is not the same as control.** If data must be processed in the EU,
  choose a model served from an EU region. Many managed models, Gemini among
  them, can be called on an EU regional or multi-region endpoint (never the
  global endpoint, which gives no residency guarantee). An endpoint's location is
  not always the whole processing boundary: Google documents that a locational
  endpoint may process data within a wider country or multi-region boundary, and
  in-country processing in Europe varies by model. So check the model's documented
  processing commitment for your requirement; where it matches, a managed endpoint
  meets the need. Self-deploy only when the requirement is stronger: the model must run on
  infrastructure you operate yourself.
- **Cost at scale is a measurement, not a rule.** A self-deployed endpoint costs
  money every hour it runs. It can beat per-use pricing only when traffic is
  steady and high enough to keep it busy, so compare the real numbers before
  moving off a managed model.

So the default is simple: **start with a managed model, and move to a
self-deployed one only for a stated need**, such as a need to run it on
infrastructure you operate, holding its weights or a measured cost saving.

## Routing between models in one agent

An agent doesn't need one model for everything. Most of its steps are simple:
deciding what kind of request it has, extracting fields, writing a short
confirmation. A few are hard: a multi-step diagnosis, a plan with trade-offs.
Send the simple steps to a small, fast model and keep the large model for the
hard ones. The router is the step that decides which model handles a request.
Agent frameworks such as <Term id="adk">ADK</Term> let each agent set its own
model, so a design can give the router and the simple steps a small model and the
reasoning step a large one.

Routing has a cost of its own: two models to evaluate, and a wrong route sends a
hard request to a model that can't handle it. Use it when the volume of simple
steps is large enough that the savings matter, and evaluate the router as
carefully as the rest of the agent.

<Callout type="note">There is no official Google rule for when to pick a small model over a large one. Treat it as engineering judgement: state the requirement, choose, and confirm with an evaluation on your own tasks.</Callout>
