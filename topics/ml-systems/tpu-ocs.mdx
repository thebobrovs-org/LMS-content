## The one-line summary

You met the <Term id="ocs">OCS</Term> in the evolution story — V4's answer to pods too
big for static copper. Here's the part that sounds like science fiction: a TPU pod
**rewires itself by tilting microscopic mirrors**. The network *is* light, and
topology is just the current mirror configuration.

## Two views of the same photons

The explorer below shows both panes at once: on the left, the **logical topology**
software sees; on the right, the **Palomar OCS** — a matrix of MEMS mirrors — that
photons see. The same pulse is drawn in both. Walk the five operations:

<Simulation id="tpu-ocs-explorer" height={1050} />

1. **Global ring** — a photon leaves a cube face, bounces off *one mirror*, lands at
   the next cube. The switch never reads the data.
2. **Pod slicing** — re-tilt the mirrors and one supercomputer becomes two. The
   slices share **zero** optical links: isolation enforced by physics, not QoS.
3. **Cross-rack jobs** — logical neighbors ≠ physical neighbors. Any healthy cubes,
   anywhere on the floor, can be warped into a contiguous slice.
4. **Spare-cube repair** — when a cube dies, drop 2 circuits, raise 2 to the hot
   spare: **4 mirror moves** and the job keeps its torus shape.
5. **Topology shuffle** — every press is a new network, born purely from mirror
   angles. Circuits are set **per job**, never per packet.

## Keeping the beam locked: the two-color trick

A fair question: how does a tilted mirror reliably hit a fiber thousands of mirror-widths
away — and *hold* it for a week-long job, through vibration and thermal drift? Apollo's
answer is a second color. The data rides **O-band signal light**; alongside it, an
**850 nm monitor beam** is injected and read by a **camera**, merged and split by a
**dichroic mirror** (one that reflects one wavelength and passes the other). The camera sees
exactly where each beam lands, and a control loop nudges the MEMS mirrors back on target —
**closed-loop alignment**. That servo is what makes an all-optical switch reliable enough to
anchor a 4,096-chip pod with no packets to retransmit.

## Why the cube is the unit

Inside a 4×4×4 cube, all 64 chips are **hard-wired electrically** — that never
changes. Only the cube's **48 optical in/out pairs** rise to the OCS layer. So
everything the OCS does — slicing, healing, reshaping — happens at **cube
granularity**. (The explorer shows 8 of 64 cubes and 1 of 6 link dimensions; the
real pod runs that picture six times in parallel, which is how it builds full — even
*twisted* — 3D toruses.)

## What it costs, what it buys

The entire OCS layer is **under 5% of system cost and ~3% of power** (per the TPU v4
paper) — and in exchange the pod gains slicing, fault-healing, and per-job topology.
That's why the evolution lesson called OCS *the only way to physically wire a
100×-pod*: a static harness of this size would fail constantly; mirrors route around
death in milliseconds.

## Why this matters

Every idea later in the path leans on this: slices of arbitrary shape (topologies),
schedulers that build them from whatever's healthy (provisioning), and Multislice
jobs that treat pods as LEGO. Once you've seen that *topology is software*, none of
those feel arbitrary.
